Why people count words in the first place

Most of the time it's a limit. School essays come with a word count, job applications ask for a cover letter under 300 words, and blog posts have a target length. Then there are the quieter reasons — a caption that has to fit, a bio for a profile, an article that needs to hit a client's minimum.

And the count actually matters. Go too far over an essay limit and marks get cut. Send a cover letter that's twice as long as asked and it looks like you didn't read the instructions. On the other hand, a post that's too short can look thin. So before you send anything with a limit attached, count it. It takes five seconds and saves an awkward redo.

What the count actually includes (and what trips people up)

The basic rule is simple: anything separated by a space counts as one word. That's why most tools agree with each other — but not always. A hyphen can split one tool's count from another's, and an email address pasted in by mistake counts as a word too.

The biggest trap is copying from a PDF. Headers, footers, and page numbers come along for the ride, and each one adds to the count. So before you trust the number, glance at what you pasted. If there's a page number or a "Page 3 of 12" hiding in there, delete it and watch the count drop to the real number.

And one more thing: if your limit is strict — say a form that rejects anything over 500 words — leave yourself a little room. Aim for 490 or so. That way a hyphen or a stray number can't push you over at the last second.

  • Hyphenated words like "well-known" usually count as one word.
  • Numbers count as words — "2026" is one word to most counters.
  • Contractions like "don't" count as one word.
  • Page numbers, headers, and footers copied from a PDF add fake words.

Counting words in Word, Google Docs, and on your phone

If your document is already open in Word, you don't need a separate tool at all. Word shows the count in the bottom-left corner — click it for characters and paragraphs too. Google Docs hides it under Tools, then Word count, or Ctrl+Shift+C.

But both of those need the file open in the app. An online counter is faster when you just have a chunk of text — a caption, a paragraph for a form, something you wrote in a notes app. Paste, read the number, done. It also works the same on your phone: open the tool in Chrome or Safari, paste, and the count is right there. No app to install, which is a nice change from the tools that make you download something just to count words.

One handy trick for long documents: if you only need the count for one section, copy just that section. You don't have to count the whole document to check one chapter.

How many words should your text be?

There's no magic number that fits everything — it depends on where the text is going. A cover letter that runs to 800 words won't get read. A blog post of 200 words looks thin next to other posts on the same topic.

A good rule of thumb: match the length to the job. If someone asked for 500 words, give them close to 500, not 200 and not 900. And if there's no limit at all, aim for the shortest version that still says what it needs to say. Readers can tell when you're padding, and a tight 600-word piece beats a stretched 1,000-word one every time.

  • Short bio or profile: 50 to 100 words.
  • Cover letter: 250 to 400 words.
  • School essay: whatever the assignment says — usually 500 to 1,500 words.
  • Blog post: 800 to 1,500 words is a solid target for most topics.
  • Social media caption: under 150 words, and shorter usually does better.

Quick ways to hit your word limit without the stress

If you're over the limit, cut the easy stuff first. Adverbs like "very" and "really" go. Long intros shrink to a sentence or two. And read each paragraph out loud — if a sentence doesn't add anything, it goes. Most people find they can cut 10% without losing a single idea.

If you're under the limit, don't pad. Add one real example, one extra detail, or one more point that actually helps the reader. A single concrete example is worth more than three fluffy sentences, and it reads better too. And if you're still short, ask yourself whether the limit is a minimum or just a suggestion — sometimes "at least 800 words" is a hard rule, and sometimes it's just a guide.

Frequently asked questions

Do word counters count the title and spaces too?

It depends on what you ask for. A plain word count is just words — most tools don't count your title unless you paste it in too. Character counts usually include spaces by default, but a lot of tools let you switch that off. If your count looks off, check whether the tool is counting the header, the footer, or the notes you pasted in by mistake.

Can I count the words in an essay or a long document?

A word counter just counts — it doesn't check grammar or fix your writing. So yes, you can paste an essay, a blog post, or a job application and get a number. Just keep in mind that most online counters work in your browser, so your text stays on your computer. If you paste something private into a tool that uploads it to a server, that's a different story — stick to browser-based tools for anything you wouldn't want shared.

Is an online word counter better than Word or Google Docs?

Word and Google Docs both count words, and they're fine. The difference is speed. With an online counter you paste and you're done — no file to open, no app to wait for, and it works on any device, even a phone. That's why people use one for a quick check: a caption, a bio, a paragraph for a form. For a 10,000-word thesis, your word processor is the better tool.

Can I count words in a PDF this way?

Yes, if you paste the right text. Copy the whole passage — not just the first paragraph — and paste it into the counter. Keep an eye out for page numbers or headers that sneak in when you copy from a PDF: those add fake words to the count. Read the number, then read the text once through to make sure nothing extra got in.

Why do two word counters give me different numbers?

Usually it's the hyphen. "Well-known" counts as one word in some tools and two in others, and the same goes for numbers like "2026" and contractions like "don't". Most tools treat anything separated by a space as one word, so hyphenated words and email addresses count as one. If two tools disagree by a word or two, that's almost always why — and for a limit like "500 words max", a couple of words either way is worth checking yourself.

Can I count words on my phone?

Yes — the counter in this guide works on phones just the same as on a computer. Open the tool in your browser, paste your text, and the count shows up right away. No app to install and nothing to download.
