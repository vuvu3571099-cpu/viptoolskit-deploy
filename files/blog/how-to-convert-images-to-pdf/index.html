You've got ten photos — receipts, homework, a signed form, a few pages of notes — and someone needs them as one PDF. Sending ten separate files is messy, and half the time one goes missing.

Good news: it takes about two minutes, and you don't need an app or an account. Here's how to do it in your browser, plus a few tricks for the order, size, and quality.

Why one PDF beats a pile of photos

A single PDF is just easier for everyone. The person on the other end opens one file and scrolls through it in order — no ten attachments to download, no guessing which photo came first, no "did you get image 7?" messages.

And PDFs look the same everywhere. A JPG opens differently on every phone and computer, but a PDF keeps your pages in the order and size you set. That's why job applications, school submissions, and expense claims all ask for PDFs — the one format nobody has to fiddle with.

Before you start: get your images in order

Two minutes of sorting now saves you redoing the whole PDF later. The converter keeps the order you drop the files in, so drop them in backwards and your PDF comes out backwards too.

This is the step most people skip, and it's the one that causes the most re-dos. Open each photo and check it's the right way up — a sideways receipt photo turns into a sideways page, and nobody enjoys tilting their head to read an expense claim.

  • Put the images in one folder, named in the order you want them — 01, 02, 03 beats IMG_4821, IMG_4822.
  • Check each image is the right way up — sideways photos become sideways pages.
  • Use JPG or PNG — the tool accepts both together.

Getting the page order and layout right

Order is the thing to get right before you hit convert, because fixing it afterwards means starting over. The tool shows a little thumbnail for each image — drag them around until the sequence reads the way you'd read the document on paper.

Page size matters more than people think. Pick a page much wider than your photo and you get big white margins; much narrower and the photo shrinks to fit. A4 is the default for a reason — it fits a phone photo nicely and prints without surprises. And if your images are a mix of tall and wide, pick portrait and let the wide ones sit a little smaller — better than a PDF that flips orientation every other page.

  • Order: the first image you drop in becomes page one — drag thumbnails to fix it before you convert.
  • Page size: A4 for documents and forms, Letter for the US.
  • Orientation: portrait for documents, wide pages for wide screenshots.

Keeping the PDF small enough to send

A PDF made from ten phone photos can easily hit 20MB or 30MB — over the attachment limit on a lot of email services. Gmail caps attachments at 25MB, and plenty of job portals cap lower.

So shrink the images before you convert them. A photo straight off a phone is often 4000 pixels wide, and you don't need that for a PDF someone reads on screen. Run them through a compressor first — down to around 1200 pixels wide, saved as JPG — and the finished PDF can shrink to a quarter of the size while still looking sharp. Still big? Check for a PNG in the batch: a PNG photo is far heavier than the same photo as a JPG, so convert it first.

Doing it on your phone

You don't need an app for this — honestly an app is the slowest way to do it, what with installing it, giving it access to your photos, then finding the file it saved. The browser tool works fine on a phone: open it in Chrome or Safari, tap to pick your photos, and the PDF downloads like any other file.

There are built-in tricks too. On an iPhone, select photos, tap Share, then Print, and pinch out on the preview to save them as a PDF. On Android, Google Photos and most galleries have a Print or Save as PDF option. But those routes hide the page order and size settings, so for anything that matters — a job application, a form, a set of receipts — the browser tool gives you more control, and works the same on both phones.

Frequently asked questions

Will my photos still look good in the PDF?

Yes — and it stays readable. The tool fits each image to a page, so a photo that was clear on your phone is still clear in the PDF. If a photo looks tiny or blurry, it was probably a small image to begin with, so start from the biggest copy.

How many images can I put in one PDF?

There's no hard limit, but big batches get slow — 100 phone photos can make a PDF of 50MB or more, and that's a pain to email. For big batches, split them into a few PDFs, or shrink the images first with a compressor.

Is it safe to convert my photos to PDF online?

The free tool in this guide runs in your browser — no account needed. Your photos never leave your computer — the PDF is made right there on your device. That's worth checking with any online tool: if it asks you to upload first and wait, your files are going to its server.

Can I check the PDF before I send it?

Yes. Open the PDF, check the first page is the one you wanted first, and flip through the rest. If a page is upside down, rotate that image and convert again — quicker to fix it now than to send the wrong version and resend it.

Why won't my PDF attach to an email?

Most likely it's just big — ten phone photos can make a 20MB or 30MB PDF, and a lot of email services cap attachments at 25MB. Shrink the images before converting, or pick a smaller page size. If it's still too big, send it through a file-sharing link instead.

Can I do this on my phone without an app?

On an iPhone, open Photos, select your images, tap Share, then Print, and pinch out on the preview — that saves them as a PDF. On Android, open Google Photos or your gallery, select the images, and look for Print or Save as PDF. But the browser tool in this guide works on both, so you don't need a different trick for each phone.
