What Is HEIC Format? (And How to Open or Convert It)

Quick answer

HEIC is Apple's default iPhone photo format. It stores the same photo in about half the file size of a JPG with no visible quality loss. The catch: Windows and many websites don't open it natively, so you either convert it to JPG or install Apple's HEIF support.

You took a photo on your iPhone, sent it to a friend on Windows, and they replied: "I can't open this." Or you tried to upload it to a website and got a flat "unsupported file type." The file ends in .heic, and suddenly a simple photo has turned into a small headache.

HEIC isn't broken, and your phone isn't doing anything wrong. It's just a newer photo format that Apple adopted years ago — and the rest of the world is still catching up. Here's what it is, why it exists, and what to do when it gets in your way.

What HEIC actually is

HEIC stands for High Efficiency Image Container. It's a photo format built on a standard called HEIF, which uses the same compression technology as modern video — the HEVC codec that powers 4K streaming. Apple started using it as the iPhone's default photo format back in 2017, with iOS 11.

The idea is simple: pack the same photo into a smaller file. A HEIC photo is usually about half the size of the same shot saved as a JPG, and on a normal screen you can't tell them apart. Your phone can fit twice as many photos, uploads are faster, and backups take less space. For a device that shoots thousands of photos, that adds up fast.

There's another neat trick hiding in HEIC. Because it's a container format, a single .heic file can hold more than one image — so it can store the live-photo video clip, several exposures of the same shot, and even depth data for portrait mode, all in one file. A JPG can't do any of that.

Why Apple switched to HEIC (and why it matters to you)

Apple didn't switch just to be different. Phone cameras were getting better every year, and photos were getting bigger. A 12-megapixel shot saved as JPG runs 3 to 5MB, and a 48-megapixel shot from a newer iPhone can hit 8MB or more. At that rate, a 128GB phone fills up fast.

HEIC was the fix. Same photo, half the size, and it keeps the extra stuff — the little video clip in a Live Photo, the depth map that makes portrait mode work — in the same file instead of scattering it across sidecar files. If you only ever live inside the Apple world, you never notice any of this. Your photos just work, and they take up less room.

And honestly, the quality is real. HEIC also supports 16-bit colour, which means smoother gradients in skies and skin tones than JPG's 8-bit colour can manage. On most screens you won't spot the difference, but photographers editing heavily will.

  • Keeps Live Photos, burst shots, and depth data in one file.
  • Supports 16-bit colour — JPG is stuck at 8-bit.

HEIC vs JPG: the honest comparison

So which one's better? It depends on what you care about. On pure quality per megabyte, HEIC wins — smaller files, same look, more colour depth. On compatibility, JPG wins by a mile. Every device, website, and app made in the last twenty years opens a JPG. HEIC still doesn't.

Here's how they stack up. A typical iPhone photo is around 2MB as HEIC and 4MB as JPG. Quality on screen: basically identical. Editing headroom: HEIC has a small edge thanks to the extra colour data. But compatibility: JPG works everywhere, HEIC works on Apple devices, newer Android phones, and Windows only after you install extra software.

For most people, the practical answer is boring but true. If you live in the Apple world and rarely share outside it, leave HEIC on — your phone stays lighter and your photos look great. If you share photos with Windows users, upload them to websites, or send them to clients, JPG saves you a conversion step every single time. There's no wrong answer, just a trade-off between file size and hassle.

Where HEIC causes problems

The trouble starts the moment a HEIC file leaves Apple's world. Windows is the biggest one — double-click a .heic file on a fresh Windows install and nothing happens. You need to install the HEIF Image Extensions from the Microsoft Store, and for most photos you also need the HEVC Video Extensions, which costs about a dollar. It works, but it's an extra step most people never expect.

Websites are the second headache. Plenty of upload forms — job portals, university applications, government sites, even some social platforms — simply reject .heic files. They were built around JPG and PNG, and nobody updated the accepted-formats list. You fill in a whole form, attach your photo, hit submit, and get "unsupported file type." This is the step most people skip checking until it's too late.

And then there's sharing. Send a HEIC photo to an Android user on an older phone, or attach it to an email, and the person on the other end may see a broken image or nothing at all. iPhones actually convert HEIC to JPG automatically when you share via some apps, which is why the problem feels random — it works in Messages, then fails in an email attachment.

None of this makes HEIC a bad format. It's just a newer one, and the rest of the world moves slowly. The gap is closing every year, but right now, compatibility is still the thing you have to plan around.

How to open or convert a HEIC file

You've got a HEIC file and something won't open it. You have three options, and the right one depends on how often this happens to you.

  1. Convert it to JPG in your browser — open the free HEIC to JPG tool (link below), drop the file in, and download a JPG. Takes a few seconds, nothing to install, and the file never leaves your computer.
  2. On Windows, install the HEIF Image Extensions from the Microsoft Store (plus the HEVC Video Extensions) so Photos and File Explorer can open HEIC files directly. It's a one-time setup.
  3. On your iPhone, stop the problem at the source: go to Settings, then Camera, then Formats, and pick Most Compatible. New photos will be saved as JPG from then on. Your old HEIC photos stay as they are.
  4. If you email photos from your iPhone a lot, check Settings, then Photos, and make sure "Automatic" is on under Transfer to Mac or PC — your phone will convert HEIC to JPG on the fly when it sends them.

Should you keep using HEIC?

If your photos mostly stay on your iPhone and in iCloud, yes — keep it. You're getting smaller files and better quality, and you never have to think about it. Apple's whole system is built around HEIC now, and it handles everything behind the scenes.

But if you regularly send photos to Windows users, upload them to websites, or work with clients who use all kinds of devices, switching your camera to Most Compatible is the simpler life. You'll lose the space savings, but you'll never hit a "can't open this file" wall again. For most people who share a lot, that's the better trade.

And whatever you choose, don't delete your HEIC originals after converting. The JPG you make from a HEIC is a normal photo and works everywhere, but the conversion is one-way — you can't get the smaller, richer original back from the JPG. Keep the original filed away, convert a copy when you need one, and you'll never be stuck.

Frequently asked questions

Is HEIC better than JPG?

Yes, for most people. On an iPhone, HEIC photos take up roughly half the space of the same photo saved as JPG, and they look just as good. The only time it's not worth it is when you share photos with people on Windows or older devices a lot — then the hassle of converting every time can outweigh the space you save.

How do I stop my iPhone from saving photos as HEIC?

Open your iPhone's Settings, go to Camera, then Formats, and pick Most Compatible. New photos will be saved as JPG from then on. Your old HEIC photos stay as they are — this only changes what the camera saves going forward.

Can Windows open HEIC files?

Not on its own, no. Windows 10 and 11 can open HEIC files, but only after you install the HEIF Image Extensions (and usually the HEVC Video Extensions too) from the Microsoft Store. The HEVC one costs about a dollar. Or skip all that and convert the file to JPG in your browser — it takes a few seconds and nothing to install.

Why won't a website accept my HEIC photo?

Yes. The file itself is fine — it's just a format most web platforms don't accept yet. Convert it to JPG first and it'll upload like any other photo. If you upload HEIC files to a website regularly, switching your iPhone camera to Most Compatible saves you the extra step every time.

What does HEIC stand for?

It's Apple's name for a photo format built on a video-compression standard called HEIF (High Efficiency Image Format). HEIC is the version of HEIF that uses HEVC compression — the same tech behind 4K video. In plain terms: it's a smarter way to pack a photo into a smaller file without losing visible quality.

Can I convert a JPG back to HEIC?

No. Converting a HEIC file to JPG is a one-way street — the JPG you get is a normal photo and won't turn back into HEIC. That's fine in practice, since JPG opens everywhere. Just keep the original HEIC file somewhere safe if you ever want the smaller, higher-quality version back.
