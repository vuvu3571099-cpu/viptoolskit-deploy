Your JPG photos are bigger than they need to be. Not a little bigger — often 25 to 35% bigger than the exact same photo saved as WebP. And on a website, that extra weight is what makes pages load slowly, especially on a phone connection.

WebP is Google's image format, and it does one job really well: it keeps your photo looking the same while shrinking the file. Converting a JPG to WebP takes about ten seconds, it's free, and you don't need to install anything. Here's how it works, what you gain, and the few times when you should stick with JPG.

Why convert JPG to WebP in the first place?

The short version: WebP was built to do JPG's job with less data. Google designed it that way, and every major browser now supports it. So if your photos live on a website, a blog, or an online shop, switching them to WebP is one of the easiest speed wins you can get.

And it's not just websites. If you email photos, upload them to a form, or back them up online, smaller files mean faster uploads and less space used. The photo itself doesn't change — only the file gets lighter.

  • Smaller files — the same photo as WebP is usually 25–35% smaller than the JPG version.
  • Faster pages — smaller images load quicker, and visitors on slow connections notice the difference.
  • Less storage — if you keep hundreds of photos, the savings add up fast.
  • Same quality — at the right settings, nobody can tell the WebP from the original JPG.
  • Better for SEO — Google rewards fast-loading pages, and images are usually the heaviest part of a page.

JPG vs WebP — what's the actual difference?

JPG has been around since the early 90s, and it does a decent job of squeezing photos down. But it was designed for a different era, when screens were small and internet was slow. WebP uses newer compression tricks, so it packs the same photo into fewer bytes.

Here's a quick comparison. A typical 2 MB holiday photo saved as JPG comes out at around 1.3 to 1.5 MB as WebP — same resolution, same look. Do that across a whole photo gallery and you've cut a third of your page weight without touching a single pixel.

One thing WebP also does that JPG can't: transparency. JPG has no transparent pixels at all, while WebP supports them like PNG does. That only matters if you need see-through backgrounds, but it's a nice bonus. The one thing JPG still wins on is universal support — every device, app, and printer on earth opens a JPG, while WebP still trips up the odd old program.

How much smaller does a WebP actually get?

It depends on the photo. A busy outdoor scene with lots of detail might shrink by 20%, while a simple product shot on a plain background can drop by 40% or more. Photos with big areas of sky, wall, or flat colour compress best, because there's less detail to store.

Here's the honest bit: you won't always hit 35%. Some JPGs are already well compressed, and those won't shrink much further. But in our testing, most everyday photos — phone snaps, product shots, blog images — land somewhere between 25 and 35% smaller, and that's a real saving when a page has ten or twenty images on it.

The file size also depends on the quality setting. Higher quality means a bigger file. For photos on a website, quality 80 to 85 is the sweet spot — small file, no visible difference. Go below 70 and you'll start to see the photo soften, so don't push it just to save a few more kilobytes.

What to watch out for before you convert

Most of these are common sense, but the first one is the step most people skip: actually looking at the result. A WebP at the wrong quality setting can look fine as a thumbnail and terrible at full size. So open it, zoom in once, and then you're done.

  • Check the quality after converting — open the WebP and zoom in. If it looks soft or blotchy, convert again at a higher quality.
  • Keep the original JPG — once you've replaced a file, there's no undo. Keep the JPGs in a backup folder until you're happy with the WebP versions.
  • Watch out for old software — some older photo editors and office programs still can't open WebP. If you're sending the file to someone else, ask first or send a JPG.
  • Don't convert tiny thumbnails — if the image is already 20 KB, converting it saves almost nothing and adds a step. Focus on the big photos.
  • Test your website after switching — swap a few images first, load the page on your phone, and check everything looks right before you convert the whole gallery.

Small tips that save you time

A couple of habits make this a ten-second job instead of a chore.

First, convert before you upload, not after. It's much easier to convert a photo on your own computer than to fix a slow page later. Make WebP part of the routine: shoot, convert, then upload.

Second, name your files clearly. If your JPG was called beach-sunset.jpg, save the WebP as beach-sunset.webp — same name, different ending. That way you always know which is which, and your website links stay easy to follow.

And if you convert images a lot — blog photos, product shots, social media posts — bookmark the tool. You'll reach for it more than you think, and every converted photo makes your pages that little bit faster.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert a JPG to WebP for free?

Open VIPToolsKit's free JPG to WebP converter, drop in your JPG file, and download the WebP version. It takes a few seconds and there's no sign-up, no download, and no watermark. You can do it on your phone or your computer.

Does converting JPG to WebP lose quality?

Not if you pick a high quality setting. WebP is built to keep the same look at a much smaller file size — most people can't tell the difference between the original JPG and the WebP side by side. If you push the compression too far, though, you'll start to see soft edges and blotchy patches, so stick to quality 80 or above for photos.

Can I convert many JPGs to WebP at once?

Yes — the converter handles one file at a time in the browser, so you can run through a whole folder of photos quickly. If you have dozens of images, convert them in batches and keep the originals until you've checked the results.

Do all browsers support WebP?

Pretty much, yes. Every modern browser — Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari (14 and up), Opera — shows WebP images. If some of your visitors still use a very old browser, keep a JPG copy of the important images as a fallback. For most sites in 2026, WebP just works.

Will WebP images work on my phone?

Yes. Android and iOS both support WebP, and your phone's gallery and browser will open the files fine. If you need to share a photo with someone whose phone or app doesn't accept WebP, convert it back to JPG first — that takes a few seconds too.

Is WebP better than JPG for my website?

For websites, yes — smaller files mean faster page loads, and faster pages rank better and keep visitors around. For archiving or printing, JPG is still the safer pick, since it's supported everywhere and nobody will ever ask you what a WebP is.
