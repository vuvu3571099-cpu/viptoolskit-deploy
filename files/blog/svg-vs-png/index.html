SVG vs PNG: When to Use Each Format (A Simple Guide)

Quick answer

Use SVG for logos, icons, and simple graphics that need to stay sharp at any size. Use PNG for photos, screenshots, and anything with lots of detail or soft shadows. If it has to look the same on a tiny phone icon and a huge screen, it's an SVG job.

You've got a logo, an icon, or a graphic to put on your website — and now you're stuck on a simple question: SVG or PNG? Pick wrong and your logo goes blurry on a big screen, or your photo turns into a giant file that loads slowly.

The good news is the choice is simple once you know the one big difference between them. An SVG is a set of instructions for drawing a shape. A PNG is a grid of coloured pixels. Here's how that plays out in real life — and which one to pick each time.

SVG vs PNG: the one difference that matters

Think of it like this. A PNG is a photo of a drawing — every pixel is fixed in place. If you zoom in, you start seeing the little squares. An SVG is more like the recipe for the drawing: "draw a circle here, a line there." The browser follows the recipe fresh every time, at whatever size you ask for.

That's why an SVG logo looks perfectly sharp on a phone and on a 4K monitor, from the exact same file. A PNG logo, on the other hand, only looks sharp at the size it was made for. Make it bigger and it goes soft and blocky — you've probably seen this on websites where a logo looks fine on desktop but fuzzy on a phone.

So the first question to ask yourself is simple: will this image ever need to change size? If yes, SVG has a big head start.

When SVG wins

And here's a bonus most people don't know about: SVG files are just text. That means you can open one in any text editor, and search engines can read what's inside. A small SVG icon can be just a few hundred bytes — smaller than the PNG version by a mile.

  • Logos. Your logo shows up everywhere — tiny in a browser tab, big in a page header. One SVG covers all of it.
  • Icons. Menu icons, social icons, little symbols next to buttons. SVGs stay crisp and their files are tiny.
  • Simple illustrations and charts. Flat shapes, clean lines, a few colours — that's SVG's home turf.
  • Anything you might need to recolour later. Change one line in an SVG and the whole graphic changes colour. Try that with a PNG and you're redrawing it.

When PNG wins

PNG also has one trick that keeps it popular: transparency. A PNG can have a see-through background, so your logo or cut-out photo sits cleanly on any colour behind it. No white box around it. That's why so many logos you download come as PNGs, even when an SVG would be sharper.

  • Photos. A photo has millions of colours and tiny details. SVG can't do that — it's the wrong tool for the job.
  • Screenshots. Text on screen, app windows, anything with fine detail and soft edges comes out clean in PNG.
  • Images with shadows and gradients. Soft drop shadows, glows, and smooth colour blends are where PNG shines and SVG struggles.
  • When you need exact pixels. PNG shows exactly what you saved, pixel for pixel. SVG gets redrawn by the browser, and very old software sometimes draws it slightly differently.

SVG vs PNG at a glance

Still not sure? Run through this quick check. If most of these point one way, you've got your answer.

  • Sharp at any size: SVG wins, PNG loses.
  • Photos and fine detail: PNG wins, SVG can't do it.
  • Small file size for simple graphics: SVG wins.
  • Transparency: both can do it.
  • Works everywhere, even in old apps: PNG wins.
  • Easy to edit and recolour: SVG wins.

Mistakes people make with SVG and PNG

The most common mistake is saving a logo as a PNG at one small size and then stretching it everywhere. It looks fine on your laptop and terrible on a big screen. If you only have a PNG logo, save it at least twice as big as the largest place it'll appear — that way it stays sharp when it gets scaled down.

The other mistake is the opposite one: forcing a photo into SVG format. Some tools will do it, but the result is a giant file that looks worse than the PNG you started with. Photos are pixels. Let them be pixels.

And one last thing: keep the original SVG of your logo safe. It's the master copy. From it, you can make a PNG at any size, any time. But a PNG can never turn back into a clean SVG, so if you lose the original, you're stuck redrawing it.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use SVG for photos?

No. SVG is made for shapes, lines, and flat colours — logos, icons, simple illustrations. A photo has millions of colours and tiny details, and trying to turn it into an SVG would make a huge file that looks worse. Photos belong in PNG or JPG.

Is SVG smaller than PNG?

Usually, yes — for simple graphics. A logo as an SVG might be 5 KB while the same logo as a PNG could be 50 KB or more. But the moment the graphic gets detailed, PNG can end up smaller. So size alone isn't the reason to pick one; the type of image is.

Do all browsers support SVG?

Yes, all modern browsers support SVG — Chrome, Firefox, Safari, Edge, and the browsers on your phone. It's been safe to use on websites for years now. The only place it still causes trouble is very old software, like ancient versions of some email apps.

Which format is better for a logo?

SVG, almost always. A logo has to look sharp on a tiny phone screen and on a big desktop monitor, and only SVG can do both from one file. Keep a PNG copy too, though, for places that only accept image files — like some social media uploads and email signatures.

Can PNG be transparent like SVG?

Yes. PNG supports transparency, so you can have a logo or a cut-out photo with no white box around it. That's one reason PNG is still so popular. The difference is that PNG transparency is baked into fixed pixels, while an SVG stays sharp at any size.
