How to Write a CV With No Experience (Free Guide + Examples)

Quick answer

Use a skills-first CV format: lead with a short personal summary, then your skills, education, and any projects or volunteer work. Keep it to one page, match the skills in the job posting, and be honest — employers hire first-timers all the time.

No job history, no internships, and now someone wants your CV. It feels like a catch-22: you need experience to get a job, and you need a job to get experience.

Here's the good news — employers hire people with no experience every single day. What they actually look for is a CV that's clear, honest, and shows you're worth a chance. And that's something you can build right now, even with an empty work history section.

This guide walks you through it, step by step. No fancy tricks, no lying — just a CV that gets read.

Why having no experience isn't the problem you think it is

First, let's clear up the fear. Hiring managers don't expect a fresh graduate to have five years of work history — they know you're starting out. What puts them off isn't a short CV, it's a messy one, a dishonest one, or one that looks copied from a template and unchanged. They're hiring for an entry-level role, so they expect entry-level candidates. They want someone who shows up, learns fast, and communicates clearly — and your CV just needs to prove those three things.

So stop worrying about the empty work history section. Build your CV around what you do have — and most first-timers have more than they think.

  • School projects, assignments, and group work all count as experience.
  • Part-time jobs, family business help, and volunteering all count too.
  • Skills you picked up in school — Excel, typing, a language, basic coding — belong on your CV.
  • The way you present it matters more than how much you have.

Use the skills-first CV format

The classic CV format leads with work history, which is the worst possible layout when you don't have any. It puts your weakest section first and makes the whole page feel empty.

So flip it. A skills-first CV leads with what you can do, then your education, then any projects or activities. There's no work history section at all — and nobody misses it, because the page looks full and useful from the first line.

One more thing: keep it to one page. A recruiter gives your CV about six seconds on the first pass, so one page that's easy to scan beats two pages of filler every time.

  • Personal summary — two or three lines about who you are and what you're looking for.
  • Skills — the ones the job actually asks for, plus a few practical ones.
  • Education — your degree or school, with grades if they're good.
  • Projects and activities — school work, volunteer work, anything real you've done.
  • Extras — languages, certificates, tools you know.

Step by step: build your CV from scratch

  1. Write down everything you've done: school projects, part-time jobs, volunteer work, family business help, online courses, anything. Don't filter yet — just list it all.
  2. Pick the 4 to 6 items that fit the job you're applying for. A project that used Excel matters more than a random hobby if the job needs Excel.
  3. Write a two-line summary about yourself. Say what you're looking for and one thing you're good at. Keep it plain and honest.
  4. List your skills — but only the ones you can actually back up. If you write "advanced Excel" and can't use a formula, it'll show in the interview.
  5. Fill in your education, then your projects and activities, using short bullet points. Each bullet should say what you did and what came of it.
  6. Save it as a PDF with a clear file name, like Your-Name-CV.pdf. Recruiters hate files called "CV final FINAL 2.pdf".

What to write in each section

This is where most first-timers get stuck. The trick is simple: say what you did, not just what you were. "Member of the debate society" tells an employer nothing. "Organized a debate event for 60 students and handled the schedule" tells them you can plan and run something.

For education, list your school or university, your degree or subjects, and the years. Add your grades if they're decent — and leave them out if they're not.

  • Personal summary: who you are, what you want, one strength. Two or three lines, max.
  • Skills: 6 to 8 skills, matched to the job posting where you can.
  • Education: school or university, degree or subjects, years, grades if they're good.
  • Projects: two or three, each with what you did and what came of it.
  • Extras: languages, certificates, and tools — only the ones that are true.

Example lines you can actually use

Sometimes the hardest part is just knowing how a good line sounds. Use these as a starting point and change the details to fit your own story — don't copy them word for word.

Notice the pattern in all of them: a simple action, then a small result. That's what makes a line feel real instead of vague. "Good communication skills" is vague. "Explained new stock procedures to five new staff members" is real — and it proves the same thing.

  • "Built a class website as part of a group project and handled the layout and testing."
  • "Worked weekends at a family shop, handling customers and daily cash records."
  • "Volunteered at a local food drive, coordinating donations from 40 households."
  • "Tutored two younger students in maths for a full school term."
  • "Completed a free online Excel course and used it to track a class budget.

Mistakes that get your CV skipped

A few mistakes will get your CV tossed before anyone reads past the first lines. The biggest one is lying. Don't invent a job, don't stretch a two-week stint into a year, and don't claim skills you don't have. It always comes out — usually in the interview, in front of the person who could have hired you.

The second one is spelling and grammar. One typo won't kill you, but three or four will. Read your CV out loud before you send it, or ask a friend to check it. This is the step most people skip, and it's the one that costs them the most.

And finally, don't send the same CV to every job. If the posting mentions teamwork and customer service, your CV should mention them too. Five minutes of tweaks makes a real difference.

  • Lying about experience or skills — it always comes out in the interview.
  • Spelling mistakes — read it out loud or get a friend to check it.
  • Sending the same CV everywhere — match a few lines to each job posting.
  • A messy layout — keep the font simple and the spacing even.
  • A bad file name — save it as Your-Name-CV.pdf, not "CV final 2.pdf".

Frequently asked questions

Can I write a CV with no work experience?

Yes. Employers read CVs from people with no work history all the time — they know first-timers exist. What matters is showing you're organized, willing to learn, and have something useful to offer. School projects, volunteer work, part-time jobs, and skills all count.

How long should my CV be if I have no experience?

One page. That's plenty when you don't have a long work history to list. A one-page CV that's clear and easy to skim beats a two-page one padded out with filler. Recruiters spend seconds on a first look, so keep it tight.

What skills should I put on my CV with no experience?

Start with the skills the job actually asks for. If the job posting mentions teamwork, communication, or a specific tool, put those in — but only if they're true. Mix in a few practical ones too: MS Office, basic Excel, typing speed, a language you speak, or any software you've used in school.

Should I include school projects on my CV?

Yes, and you should. A school project shows you can finish something, work with others, and solve problems — that's exactly what employers want to see. Describe what you did, not just the project name. One line about your role and one line about the result is enough.

What if I have literally nothing to put on my CV?

Don't lie — it always comes out, usually in the interview. But don't undersell yourself either. A weekend job, helping at a family shop, tutoring a classmate, or running a small online page are all real experience. Frame them honestly and they count.
