You've got a PDF that needs to be under 100KB, and the form you're filling in won't take anything bigger. Maybe it's a job application, a government portal, or an exam registration — and your PDF is sitting at 2MB, 5MB, or worse. The upload button just says "file too large" and gives you nothing to work with.

100KB is a tight limit, no doubt about it. But most PDFs can get there, and you don't need to install anything or pay anyone to do it. Here's what makes a PDF big, how to shrink it down step by step, and what to try when the file just won't budge.

Why 100KB is such a tough limit

100KB sounds small, and it is. A single photo from your phone is often 2MB to 5MB — that's 20 to 50 times the limit. So if your PDF has even a couple of photos in it, you're starting a long way from the target.

And yet 100KB is one of the most common limits out there. Government portals, exam boards, and job sites love it. They set it because small files are cheap to store and quick to process on their end. Fair enough for them — but it leaves you with the job of squeezing a big file into a tiny box.

The good news is that a PDF is mostly empty space you can't see. Big fonts get embedded, images hide at full resolution, and a scanned page carries far more detail than anyone will ever read on screen. Strip that excess away and a PDF can shrink by 90% or more without looking any different.

Why your PDF is so big in the first place

Before you compress anything, it helps to know what's actually eating up the space. In most PDFs, the answer is images. A photo dropped into a document keeps its full resolution, even if it only shows up as a small picture on the page. One 3MB photo inside a PDF is enough to blow past the 100KB limit twenty times over.

Scanned PDFs are the worst offenders. Every page is a photo, so a ten-page scan can easily be 5MB or more. Text PDFs are the opposite — a ten-page document made of plain text might be 100KB before you even compress it.

Then there's the stuff you can't see: fonts embedded in the file, old versions of pages left over from edits, and metadata. A compressor cleans most of that out automatically, which is why the first pass often makes the biggest difference.

  • Images inside the PDF saved at full resolution — the biggest culprit by far.
  • Embedded fonts, especially if the file uses several font styles.
  • Scanned pages, which are really just big photos of paper.
  • Hidden data like old edits, comments, and metadata that never got cleaned out.

What to do when your PDF is still over 100KB

Sometimes one pass isn't enough, and that's normal. Here's what to try next, in order.

First, compress the same file again. Run the already-compressed PDF through the tool a second time at a stronger setting. The second pass usually takes another big chunk off, because the first pass leaves room to go further.

Second, shrink the images inside the file. If your PDF has photos, pull them out, compress each one to a smaller size, and put them back in. Or simpler: if a photo isn't needed, delete it from the document before compressing. A single unnecessary photo can be the whole reason the file won't fit.

Third, split the PDF. If it's a long document, break it into two or three smaller files. This is the step most people skip, and it's the one that works when nothing else does. A 30-page PDF at 100KB per part becomes three files that each pass the limit — and most portals let you upload more than one file.

And last, check if the file really needs to be a PDF. If the form also accepts a JPG, a one-page document saved as a compressed image can be far smaller than the same page as a PDF. It's a workaround, but it gets the job done when the limit is strict.

Keeping the PDF readable after compression

The biggest mistake people make is crushing the file to the lowest quality in one go. Text goes fuzzy, photos turn blocky, and the document looks like a bad photocopy. Work down in steps instead — medium first, then strong — and stop the moment the size dips under 100KB.

Before you upload the compressed file, open it and zoom in on the small print. Headings and big text survive compression easily. It's the tiny footnotes, signatures, and table numbers that go first. If you can't read something, go back and compress one notch less, then trim a photo or split the file instead.

And keep a copy of the original. Once a PDF is compressed, the detail it lost is gone for good. Save the small version under a different name and leave the original untouched, so you can always start over with different settings.

  • Compress in steps and stop as soon as you hit 100KB.
  • Zoom in and check small text before you send the file anywhere.
  • Always keep the original PDF and save the small version separately.

When 100KB is the wrong target

Not every upload needs to be 100KB. If the form lets you upload 2MB, don't squeeze your PDF down to 100KB anyway — you're throwing away quality for no reason. Match the limit you're actually given, not the smallest number you've heard.

And if the PDF is going to be printed, don't compress it hard at all. A 100KB file looks fine on a screen but can look rough on paper, where every pixel counts. Keep the full-size original for anything that ends up printed.

But for online forms, job applications, and exam portals, 100KB is a real target you'll keep running into. Once you know the levers — compress, then compress again, then split if you have to — you'll never get stuck on a "file too large" error again.

Frequently asked questions

Can any PDF be compressed to 100KB?

Not every PDF can get there, honestly. A one-page text PDF will drop under 100KB without breaking a sweat. A 20-page document full of photos or a scanned PDF might not reach 100KB no matter what you try — there's just too much in it. If the file is mostly text, you'll almost always make it. If it's mostly images or scans, you may need to split it or accept a bigger limit.

Will compressing my PDF to 100KB make it blurry?

It can, if you go too far. A PDF squeezed to the lowest quality setting can turn text fuzzy and photos blocky. The fix is to compress in steps instead of jumping to the smallest setting right away. Stop as soon as the file dips under 100KB — most PDFs get there well before the quality turns bad.

How do I check my PDF's file size?

On Windows, right-click the file and pick Properties — the size is right there. On Mac, right-click and pick Get Info. On your phone, long-press the file in your Files app and check its details. If your PDF is 2MB, you've got about 20 times too much file to fit under 100KB, so expect to compress hard.

Is it safe to compress a PDF with a free online tool?

It depends on the tool. A compressor that runs in your browser never uploads your file anywhere — it does the work on your own computer, so your document stays private. That's the kind to use for anything with personal details in it. If a tool asks you to wait while it uploads, your file is going to their server, so check their privacy page first.

Why is my scanned PDF so hard to shrink?

Scanned PDFs are really just photos of pages, and photos are heavy. A single scanned page can be 500KB on its own, so a ten-page scan starts at 5MB. To shrink one, you need to lower the image quality inside the PDF and drop the resolution of the scans — the compressor in this guide does both. But fair warning: a long scanned document may still not reach 100KB, and splitting it into smaller parts is often the only way.

What's the smallest a PDF can go?

There's no fixed floor — it depends on what's in the file. A one-page PDF with a few lines of text can be as small as 10KB. A PDF with photos will bottom out much higher, because every image inside it takes up space. If your PDF has even one full-page photo, getting under 100KB means shrinking that photo a lot, and at some point the picture starts to look rough.
