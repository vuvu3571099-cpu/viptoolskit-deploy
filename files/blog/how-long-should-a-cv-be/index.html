How Long Should a CV Be? (The Honest Answer)

Quick answer

One page if you're a student or have under 5 years of experience. Two pages once you've got 5–10 years behind you or you're applying for a senior role. Three pages only for academics or very senior executives — and even then, keep it tight.

You sit down to write your CV, and the first question hits you before you've typed a word: how long should this thing actually be? One page? Two? You've heard both answers, and both sound right — which is exactly why it's so confusing.

Here's the short version: for most people, one page is enough, and two pages is the max. But the real answer depends on how much experience you've got — and, honestly, on how good you are at cutting the stuff that doesn't matter.

The short answer: one page, or two at most

Recruiters spend about six or seven seconds on a CV the first time they see it. They don't read it, they scan it — job titles, dates, a few bold words — and then they decide. A CV that fits on one or two pages gets read. A five-page one gets skimmed at best and skipped at worst.

So length isn't really about rules. It's about respect for the reader's time. If you can say it in one page, do. If you genuinely need two, that's fine too. What kills a CV is length without a reason — pages full of stuff nobody asked for.

How long should your CV be at each stage

Students and fresh graduates: one page. You don't have ten years of jobs, so don't pad it. Projects, internships, part-time work, and skills fill a page fine — and one tight page looks far better than two stretched ones.

One to five years of experience: still one page, usually. Two short job entries plus education and skills fit if you keep each bullet tight. If you're pushing onto page two with only two years of work behind you, something needs cutting.

Five to ten years: two pages is normal now. You've got enough real work to fill it. Just keep older roles to one or two lines each — your job from eight years ago doesn't need five bullets.

Ten-plus years and senior roles: two pages, three at the absolute most. Cut anything older than ten to fifteen years down to a line, or drop it. Nobody hires you for what you did in 2009.

And the one real exception: academic and research CVs. Those run longer because publications, grants, and teaching are the whole point. If that's not you, the two-page ceiling stands.

How to trim a CV that's too long

Most CVs that run to three or four pages don't have too much experience — they have too much filler. Here's where to cut first.

Start with the easy wins, then get stricter. You'll be surprised how fast a bloated CV shrinks.

  1. Cut jobs older than ten years to a single line: title, company, dates. Done.
  2. Kill the objective statement. A two-line summary at the top does the same job.
  3. One bullet per achievement, not a paragraph per job. Start each bullet with a strong verb — built, led, cut, grew.
  4. Drop skills nobody needs to see. Listing Microsoft Word in 2026 tells the reader nothing — everyone can use Word.
  5. Delete "References available on request." Everyone knows that. It's a wasted line.
  6. Shrink the margins and font a touch, but don't go below 10.5pt. If it's still too long after that, the problem is the content, not the formatting.

What if your CV is too short?

A half-empty page looks worse than a short one. If you're writing your first CV and the page looks bare, fill it honestly: coursework projects, volunteer work, part-time jobs, freelance gigs, certificates. A university project where you built something real counts — describe what you did, not just the course name.

But don't fake it with huge fonts and giant margins. Recruiters spot that in a second, and it makes the CV look emptier, not fuller. A clean, honest one-pager beats a padded two-pager every time.

Making a two-page CV easy to read

Two pages is fine — but only if the recruiter actually turns the page. Put your best stuff on page one: your latest role, your biggest wins, your key skills. If they never read page two, they should still have seen the good stuff.

Keep each job to three to five bullets max, newest first. Use clear headings and white space — a wall of text on page two gets skipped, no matter how good it is.

And check the page break before you send it. Don't let one lonely bullet spill onto page two all by itself. If it does, trim a line above until the break lands clean.

CV length mistakes that cost people interviews

The pattern is always the same: people treat CV length like a test they can pass by adding more. It's the opposite. Every extra line is a chance for the recruiter's eyes to glaze over and move on.

So before you send your CV, ask one question: does every line earn its place? If a bullet doesn't show a skill or a result the employer cares about, cut it. Your CV will be shorter — and it'll get read.

  • Sending a four-page CV for a junior role. It screams "I can't prioritise."
  • Shrinking the font to 8pt to force two pages into one. Nobody reads that.
  • Using the same length for every job. Tailor it — a short, targeted CV beats a long generic one.
  • Listing every job you've ever had, including the two-week summer job from years ago.

Frequently asked questions

Is a one-page CV always better?

Not always. One page is best when you're a student or early in your career. But if you've got eight solid years of experience, squeezing it onto one page means cutting good stuff out. Two pages is fine — the problem is never two pages, it's four.

Can a CV be three pages?

Only in rare cases: senior executives, academics, doctors, and researchers. For most jobs, three pages means you haven't edited hard enough. If your CV is three pages and you're not in one of those fields, start cutting.

Will a two-page CV hurt my chances?

No. Recruiters read two-page CVs all day long. What hurts you is a CV that's long and boring — page one decides whether they ever read page two, so put your best stuff up top.

How long should a fresh graduate's CV be?

One page. Focus on projects, internships, part-time work, and skills. One strong page beats two padded ones — and recruiters can spot padding from a mile away.

Should I list every job I've ever had?

No. Keep the last ten years or so, plus any older job that's directly relevant to the role you're applying for. Older jobs get one line each — title, company, dates — or get dropped completely.

How do I cut my CV from four pages down to two?

Start with jobs older than ten years and cut them to one line each. Then trim every role's bullets down to the three strongest. Then delete the objective statement, the references line, and any skill that isn't relevant to this job. Most four-page CVs lose a page and a half to that alone.
