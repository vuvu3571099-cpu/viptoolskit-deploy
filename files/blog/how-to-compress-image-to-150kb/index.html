How to Compress an Image to 150KB (Without Losing the Detail)

Quick answer

Resize the photo to around 800 to 1000 pixels wide, save it as a JPG, and set the quality to about 75 to 85. Most phone photos land under 150KB at those settings. If it's still a bit big, lower the quality a little at a time until it slips under the limit.

You took a nice photo, tried to upload it, and the form said no: "file size must be less than 150KB." And the photo on your phone is 3MB — twenty times too big. Job portals, exam forms, and profile pages all love this limit, and none of them tell you how to hit it.

Good news: 150KB is a generous limit. You don't have to crush your photo down to a thumbnail to get there — you just need to trim the size and the quality a little, and it takes about a minute.

Why so many sites ask for 150KB

If you've ever uploaded a profile photo to a job portal, registered for an exam, or submitted a photo to a government form, you've probably met this number. 150KB is the classic middle-ground limit — big enough for a clear photo, small enough that the site can store millions of them without choking.

Phone cameras don't help. A single photo runs 2MB to 6MB, so the file you need is almost always far too big straight out of the camera. And shrinking it in your phone's gallery usually doesn't give you a number to aim at — you need a tool that shows you the file size as you go.

What actually makes an image file smaller

Three things decide how big an image file is: the format, the dimensions, and the quality setting. At 150KB you don't have to push any of them hard — you just need them working together.

Dimensions come first because they decide how many pixels the file has to store. A photo that's 4000 pixels wide holds sixteen times the pixels of one that's 1000 wide. But the form shows your photo at maybe 500 pixels wide, so those extra pixels are just dead weight. Cutting the width down to 800 or 1000 can drop a 3MB photo to under 500KB before you even touch the quality slider.

Format comes next. A JPG of a photo is far smaller than a PNG of the same photo, because JPG drops tiny details your eyes can't see anyway. PNG keeps every pixel perfect, which is great for screenshots and logos — and terrible for file size. If your file is a PNG, convert it to JPG first and watch the size fall.

Then there's quality. At quality 90 a JPG keeps almost everything; at 80 it drops details you can't see at normal sizes, and the file can shrink by half again. At 150KB you'll work in the 75 to 85 range, and that's the sweet spot — the photo still looks sharp, and the file is small enough for any form.

  • Dimensions: the width and height in pixels — the biggest lever you have.
  • Format: JPG for photos; PNG makes much bigger files for the same picture.
  • Quality: 75–85 is the range that usually lands under 150KB.

Getting to 150KB without wrecking the photo

The biggest mistake is crushing the quality slider while the photo is still full-size. A 4000-pixel photo at quality 50 still won't be 150KB — and it'll look worse than it needs to. Resize first: once the photo is 1000 pixels wide, quality 80 will usually land you under 150KB.

Work down in small steps — 85, then 80, then 75 — and stop the moment the file dips under 150KB. There's no prize for going smaller than the limit, and every step down costs you a little detail.

And keep a copy of the original. Once you compress a photo, the detail it lost is gone for good, so save the small version under a different name. You might need the big one later — for printing, or for a form with a bigger limit.

  • Resize first, then touch quality — doing it the other way round wastes your time.
  • Lower the quality in small steps and stop as soon as you hit 150KB.
  • Always keep the original file, and save the compressed copy separately.

Why your image is still over 150KB

Sometimes you follow the steps and the file just won't budge. Here's what's usually going on.

First, check the format. If your file is a PNG, it'll fight you — PNGs barely shrink no matter how far you drag the slider, because the format doesn't discard detail the way JPG does. If the image is a photo, convert it to JPG and watch the size fall. If it has to stay PNG — a logo, a screenshot with text — shrinking the dimensions is your only real lever.

Second, look at the dimensions. If you skipped the resize step, no quality setting will save you — a photo that's still 4000 pixels wide holds far too many pixels for a 150KB file. Bring the width down to 1000 or so and try again.

Third, some photos are just busy — a crowd scene, a forest, a photo full of fine detail. Those compress worse than a simple headshot, so you'll need a smaller width and a lower quality setting. And if it's a scanned document, scan it in black and white — it compresses far smaller than colour.

When 150KB isn't the right target

Don't shrink every photo down to 150KB just because you can — match the limit you're actually given, not the smallest number you've heard.

And never use a 150KB file for anything that gets printed. It looks fine in a small box on a screen, but soft and blurry on paper, where every pixel counts. Keep the full-size original for print.

But for online forms, job portals, and profile photos that cap uploads at 150KB, this is all you need: resize, save as JPG, and ease the quality down until the file slips under the limit.

Frequently asked questions

Will my image still look good at 150KB?

Yes, easily — for anything shown on a screen. A photo at 150KB is still sharp at the size it shows on a form, a profile page, or a blog post. You only run into trouble if you need to print it large or crop it tightly, so keep the original file for that.

What size and quality settings should I use for 150KB?

Try around 800 to 1000 pixels wide for a photo, then set the quality to 75 to 85. Most phone photos land under 150KB at those settings. If the file is still a bit big, nudge the quality down to 70 or shrink the width a little more — small steps, and check the size each time.

Why is my image still over 150KB after compressing?

Check the format first. If the file is a PNG, convert it to JPG — a PNG photo can be three or four times the size of the same photo as a JPG, and dragging the quality slider barely helps. Also check the width: a photo straight off a phone is often 4000 pixels wide, and quality alone can't fix that.

Is 150KB small enough for a job portal or a website?

For a profile photo on a job portal or a social media upload, 150KB is fine and you can stop there. For a blog post or a website, it depends on the page — a few 150KB images are fine, but a page full of them gets slow, so aim lower for those. And never send a 150KB file to print — keep the full-size original.

Is it safe to compress a passport photo online?

Yes — the compressor in this guide runs entirely in your browser. Your photo never leaves your computer, so nothing gets uploaded to a server. That's worth checking with any online tool, especially for passport photos: if it asks you to wait for an upload, your file is going to their server.

How small should I make a passport photo for a 150KB limit?

If the form gives you exact dimensions, use those — something like 200 x 230 pixels for a passport photo. If it only gives a file-size limit, around 600 to 800 pixels wide is a good starting point: the photo stays clear at the size the form shows it, and the file drops under 150KB without much effort.
