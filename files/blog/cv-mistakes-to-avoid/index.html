Common CV Mistakes to Avoid (And How to Fix Them)

Quick answer

The biggest CV mistakes are typos, sending the same generic CV to every job, listing duties instead of results, and making it too long or hard to read. Fix those four and your CV is already ahead of most of the pile.

By VIPToolsKit Team · Updated 2026-11-22 · 6 min read

Most CVs don't get rejected because the person behind them isn't good enough. They get rejected because of small, fixable mistakes — a typo in the first line, a wall of text nobody reads, or a CV that looks like it was sent to fifty other companies too.

The good news is that these mistakes are easy to fix once you know what they are. Here's a rundown of the ones recruiters see every single day, and what to do instead.

Typos, grammar slips, and sloppy details

This is the one that kills the most applications, and it's also the easiest to avoid. A recruiter reads hundreds of CVs, and a spelling mistake in the first few lines is all it takes for yours to land in the "no" pile. It sounds harsh, but think about it from their side: if you can't be bothered to check your own CV, why would they trust you with their work?

The worst version of this is claiming you're "detail-oriented" right next to a typo. Recruiters notice that one every time, and it gets passed around the office. So before you send anything, run spellcheck, then read the whole thing out loud — you'll catch mistakes your eyes skip over on screen. And if you can, get a friend to read it too. Fresh eyes catch things you won't, because you already know what you meant to write.

Also check the small stuff people forget: your phone number, your email address, and any links. A wrong digit in your phone number means the recruiter literally can't reach you — and they won't try to find you another way. One quick check at the end saves all of that.

Sending the same CV to every job

This is the step most people skip, and it's the one that costs them the most interviews. A generic CV that could go to any company tells the recruiter you didn't really care about this job in particular — and they can tell, even if you can't see how.

You don't need to rewrite the whole thing for every application. That's overkill. But do these three things each time: read the job ad properly, pull out the three or four skills it keeps mentioning, and make sure those exact skills show up near the top of your CV. If the ad asks for Excel and customer service, and your CV leads with graphic design, you've got the wrong version in front of them.

Same goes for the job titles and the order of your bullet points. Put the most relevant experience first, even if it's not your most recent job. The recruiter is scanning for a match with their ad, so make the match easy to find.

Listing duties instead of results

Here's the difference that changes everything. "Responsible for handling customer complaints" is a duty — it says what you were supposed to do, not what actually happened. "Resolved 30+ customer complaints a week, with a 95% satisfaction rating" is a result, and it tells the recruiter you were actually good at it.

Most CVs are full of duties because they're easier to write. But recruiters have seen the same duty lists a thousand times, and they all blur together. Results stand out because they're specific and they prove something. You don't need huge numbers — small ones work fine. "Trained three new staff members" beats "helped with training" every time.

Go through your CV and look for every line that starts with "responsible for" or "duties included". Rewrite each one with a number or a clear outcome. If you can't think of a number, describe what changed because of your work — faster, cheaper, fewer complaints, happier customers. Something concrete, not just a job description copied from the ad.

  • Duty: "Responsible for social media." Result: "Grew the company Instagram from 500 to 3,000 followers in six months."
  • Duty: "Handled stock." Result: "Cut stock errors by half by setting up a simple weekly check."
  • Duty: "Worked in a team." Result: "Led a team of four on a project that finished two weeks early."

Making it too long — or cramming too much in

A CV isn't your life story. It's a sales pitch, and sales pitches are short. For most people, two pages is the limit, and one page is plenty if you're early in your career. Anything longer and the recruiter starts skimming — or stops reading altogether.

The usual reason CVs run long is old stuff that doesn't belong there anymore. Your school results from ten years ago, the part-time job you had at university, a list of every single skill you've ever touched — none of that helps you get the job in front of you. Cut it. If a line doesn't make a recruiter more likely to call you, it doesn't earn its place.

And if you're struggling to fit on two pages, check your spacing first. Big gaps between sections, huge margins, and a font size meant for a poster all eat space. Tighten those up before you start cutting real content. But don't go the other way either — tiny text crammed onto one page is just as bad. If it needs two pages, take two pages.

Design mistakes that make it hard to read

Recruiters spend about six seconds on the first scan of a CV. Six seconds. So if yours is a wall of unbroken text, or the headings are buried, or the font is something fancy that hurts to read, they've moved on before they found your best stuff.

Keep it simple. One clean font, clear headings, short paragraphs, and bullet points for your achievements. Leave white space between sections — it makes the whole thing easier to scan and it makes you look organised without trying. And stick to one or two colours at most. A CV that looks like a rainbow isn't creative, it's distracting.

One more thing: save it as a PDF. Word files can shift around on different computers, and a CV that looks perfect on your screen can arrive as a mess on theirs. PDF keeps it exactly as you made it. Just make sure the file name is sensible — "YourName-CV.pdf", not "CV-final-v3-updated.pdf".

Stretching the truth (and other trust-breakers)

This one is simple: don't lie on your CV. Not about your degree, not about your dates, not about a job title. Employers check, more often than people think, and getting caught — even after you've started the job — can end it on the spot. It's not worth the risk for a line that probably wasn't going to get you the job anyway.

That said, there's a difference between lying and selling yourself well. Saying you "led a project" when you were one of five people on it is stretching it. Saying you "contributed to a project that hit its target" is honest and still sounds good. Use the truth, but pick the version of the truth that shows your best side.

And while we're on trust: keep your personal details to the basics. Name, phone, email, city — that's enough. Nobody needs your full home address, your date of birth, or your marital status on a CV, and in a lot of places that information can actually work against you. Leave it off.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common CV mistake?

Typos. A recruiter can forgive a lot, but a spelling mistake in the first few lines makes your whole CV look careless — and if you say you're "detail-oriented" with a typo right next to it, that's the end of it. Run spellcheck, then read it out loud once more before you send it.

How long should a CV be?

For most people, two pages is the sweet spot, and one page is fine if you're early in your career. Recruiters spend seconds on a first scan, so a three-page CV full of old jobs and hobbies is working against you. Cut anything that doesn't help you get this particular job.

Should I include a photo on my CV?

In most countries, no — and in some places it can actually work against you, because it invites bias. The exceptions are jobs where a photo is normal, like modelling or acting. For a normal office job, skip the photo and use the space for something that helps you: a skill, a result, a link to your work.

Is it okay to have gaps in my CV?

Yes, gaps are normal — people take time off, study, look after family, or just take a break between jobs. Don't try to hide a gap by fudging dates; that's the thing that actually gets you in trouble. If the gap was short, it probably needs no explanation at all. If it was longer, one honest line is enough: "Career break — family care, 2024."

Should I list every job I've ever had?

No. Once you have a few years of experience, drop the short-term jobs and the ones that have nothing to do with the role you're applying for. Your student part-time job from five years ago isn't helping your application for a marketing role — but the two years you spent doing actual marketing work is. Keep the recent and the relevant; leave the rest out.

Should my CV start with a personal summary?

Yes, but only a little. A one-line summary at the top that says what you do and what you're aiming for is enough — two or three lines at most. A full paragraph about your career dreams reads like filler, and recruiters skip it. Keep it short, specific, and about the job in front of you.
