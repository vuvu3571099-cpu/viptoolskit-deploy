What is title case?

Title case is the style you see on most book titles, article headings, and movie names. The first letter of most words is capitalized, like this: "How to Train Your Dog" or "The Best Pizza in Town". It makes a heading look finished and professional, and it helps readers spot the start of each word at a glance.

It's different from sentence case, where only the first word gets a capital — "How to train your dog" — and from ALL CAPS, which feels like shouting. Title case sits in the middle: neat, readable, and the standard pick for headings almost everywhere.

The tricky part is that not every word gets capitalized. Small words — "a", "an", "the", "and", "of", "in", "on" — usually stay lowercase in the middle of a title. That's the bit people get wrong most often, and it's exactly what a converter handles for you.

  • The first and last words of a title are always capitalized, no matter what they are.
  • Main words — nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs — always get a capital letter.
  • Small words like "a", "an", "the", "and", "but", "or", "for", "of", "in", "on", "at", and "to" usually stay lowercase in the middle.
  • Words after a colon are usually capitalized, since they start a new part of the title.
  • Hyphenated words capitalize both parts: "Well-Known", "Follow-Up".

Title case rules (the ones that actually matter)

There isn't one single rulebook for title case — different style guides disagree on the small details. But the everyday version most people use follows a simple pattern, and if you follow it, your headings will look right to just about everyone.

The main rule: capitalize the big words, leave the little ones alone. Nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs get capitals. Short articles and prepositions stay lowercase. So "the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog" becomes "The Quick Brown Fox Jumps Over the Lazy Dog". Notice "the" is lowercase in the middle but capitalized at the start — that's how it works.

And one more thing worth knowing: some style guides capitalize every word, including the small ones. You see that a lot on YouTube and social media. It's not wrong, exactly — it's just a different style. The converter on this page follows the standard version with small words lowercase, which is the safer choice for blogs, documents, and schoolwork.

Title case vs sentence case vs uppercase

These three get mixed up a lot, so here's the quick difference. Title case capitalizes most words: "How to Bake a Cake". Sentence case capitalizes only the first word: "How to bake a cake". Uppercase capitalizes everything: "HOW TO BAKE A CAKE".

So which one should you use? Title case is the standard for headings, titles, and names of things — books, movies, articles, slides. Sentence case is the standard for regular sentences and for headings in some academic styles. Uppercase is best saved for short labels and warnings, because a whole paragraph in caps is hard to read.

And yes, you can switch between them in seconds. If you pasted a title in sentence case and your blog wants title case, run it through the converter instead of retyping it. One click, done.

  • Title case — "How to Bake a Cake" — best for headings and titles.
  • Sentence case — "How to bake a cake" — best for regular sentences and some academic headings.
  • Uppercase — "HOW TO BAKE A CAKE" — best for short labels only, never long text.
  • Lowercase — "how to bake a cake" — fine for casual text, but looks unfinished as a heading.

Common mistakes with title case

The most common mistake is capitalizing every single word. "How To Bake A Cake" looks close, but the "to" and "a" should be lowercase in standard title case. It's a small thing, but readers who know the rules will notice. The converter gets this right for you, so you don't have to memorize the list of small words.

The second mistake is the opposite: forgetting to capitalize a word that needs it. Verbs and adjectives always get capitals, even short ones. "How to Make a Good First Impression" — "make" and "good" and "first" all need capitals, even though they're short words. Length doesn't decide it; the type of word does.

And the third mistake is being inconsistent. If one heading on your page uses title case and the next one uses sentence case, the page looks sloppy. Pick one style for all your headings and stick with it. Running all your headings through the same converter is the easiest way to keep them matching.

Where title case matters most

Blog post titles are the big one. A title in proper title case looks more professional in search results and on social media, and people are more likely to click it. A title in all lowercase looks unfinished, and a title in ALL CAPS looks like spam. Title case is the safe middle ground.

It's also the standard for CV headings, presentation slides, and YouTube video titles. Recruiters and readers expect it, even if they don't think about it consciously. A CV section heading like "Work Experience" just looks right — "work experience" doesn't.
